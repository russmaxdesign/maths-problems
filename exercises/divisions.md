## Stage 3: learn a division decomposition method

For larger numbers, I think this could become very useful.

For example:

96 ÷ 4

could become:

80 ÷ 4 = 20
16 ÷ 4 = 4

20 + 4 = 24

96 ÷ 4 = 24

Or:

75 ÷ 5

50 ÷ 5 = 10
25 ÷ 5 = 5

10 + 5 = 15

75 ÷ 5 = 15

This may turn out to be your division equivalent of the Jump Strategy.

The principle would be:

split the dividend into friendly chunks that are easy to divide.

Not necessarily tens and units rigidly. Choose whatever is easy.

For example:

84 ÷ 6

might be:

60 ÷ 6 = 10
24 ÷ 6 = 4

10 + 4 = 14

That's very compatible with what you've already learned about yourself.

## Stage 4: three-digit dividends

Only after the two-digit examples feel comfortable.

For example:

120 ÷ 4
150 ÷ 5
240 ÷ 6
360 ÷ 8
525 ÷ 5
672 ÷ 7

And keep decomposition explicit:

672 ÷ 7 = ?

630 ÷ 7 = 90
42 ÷ 7 = 6

90 + 6 = 96

672 ÷ 7 = 96

Notice that I chose 630 + 42, not necessarily 600 + 72.

The rule should be:

choose chunks that divide cleanly.

That's more important than following place value mechanically.

---

## Stage 5: division that produces halves and quarters

You have already encountered these:

9 ÷ 2 = 4.5
14 ÷ 4 = 3.5
18 ÷ 4 = 4.5

But I wouldn't mix these in immediately.

First lock in whole-number division.

Then have a dedicated set for:

5 ÷ 2
7 ÷ 2
9 ÷ 2

6 ÷ 4
10 ÷ 4
14 ÷ 4

That lets you build the .5, .25, .75 patterns separately.

---

## Stage 6: two-digit divisors

Only later:

60 ÷ 12
84 ÷ 14
96 ÷ 12
120 ÷ 15
144 ÷ 12

Again I'd initially approach these through multiplication:

96 ÷ 12 = ?

12 × ? = 96

12 × 8 = 96

Therefore:
96 ÷ 12 = 8

I wouldn't start with long division yet.

There is no reason to introduce the formal long-division algorithm until we know whether you actually need it.
